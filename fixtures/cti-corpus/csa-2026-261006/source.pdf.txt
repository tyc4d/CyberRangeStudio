        Introduction
        This joint cybersecurity advisory was developed by the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) and the U.S.
        Secret Service (USSS) to provide information and disseminate indicators of compromise (IOCs) related to
        the ongoing FortiBleed compromise. FortiBleed is an active, global credential-compromise campaign
        targeting internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and secure socket layer (SSL) virtual private network
        (VPN) gateways, with SOCRadar verifying more than 86,644 compromised devices across 194 countries2.
        The campaign exploits reused or leaked credentials and legacy SHA-256 password storage, enabling threat
        actors to harvest and crack authentication data at scale. Initial findings indicate attackers are continuing to
        scan internet-exposed Fortinet firewalls using previously obtained compromised credentials. Affected
        organizations may find themselves locked out of their systems if threat actors disable accounts or change
        passwords, requiring remediation steps beyond standard patching and password resets. In addition, the
        FortiBleed attack chain has been observed as an initial entry point for ransomware affiliates. The FBI and
        USSS encourage organizations to implement the mitigations outlined in this advisory to help minimize the
        potential for impact from the FortiBleed attack chain.

        Technical Details
        Note: This advisory uses the MITRE ATT&CK® Matrix for Enterprise framework, version 19.1. See the MITRE
        ATT&CK Tactics and Techniques section of this advisory table of the threat actors’ activity mapped to
        MITRE ATT&CK tactics and techniques.

        Overview
        FortiBleed refers to a credential-harvesting and access-broker operation whose internal workflow became
        visible only after the threat actors unintentionally exposed their own backend server. The open directory
        revealed a mature, multi-stage campaign targeting FortiGate SSL VPN appliances, harvesting credentials at
        scale, and cracking them using a distributed graphics processing unit (GPU) cluster. The recovered tooling
        and datasets provide a rare, end-to-end view of how the operators identified targets, validated stolen
        credentials, and expanded access inside victim networks. The following points summarize the attackers’
        progression based on the artifacts left behind.3
             ▪   Attackers began by scanning [T1595] the internet for exposed [T1190] FortiGate SSL VPN portals,
                 using automated scripts to identify reachable devices and harvestable authentication surfaces.
             ▪   They collected large volumes of credentials and authentication artifacts using credential stuffing
                 [T1110.004] and password spraying [T1110.003] attacks based on prior Fortinet leak dumps and
                 infostealer logs.
             ▪   Exfiltrated [T1041] password hashes obtained via credential dumping [T1003] were funneled into
                 a GPU-accelerated cracking cluster to perform offline password cracking [T1110.002],where

                 Hashcat and Hashtopolis1 orchestrated distributed jobs to convert stolen data into usable plaintext
                 credentials.
             ▪   Cracked credentials were enriched, sorted, and validated, with scripts filtering out honeypots,
                 mapping organizations, and prioritizing high-value targets based on revenue and network structure.

             ▪   With verified credentials [T1078] in hand, attackers moved into victim environments, conducting
                 Active Directory enumeration [T1087] and password spraying to expand access and identify
                 privileged accounts.
             ▪   The operation culminated in packaging and selling access, with working VPN configurations and
                 target lists indicating the group’s role as an initial-access broker supplying compromised networks
                 to downstream threat actors.
        Potential Lockout
        Based on initial responses, some victims may get locked out of their Fortinet devices if the threat actor
        either deletes or changes the password for original accounts on the system [T1531]. During the initial
        intrusion, threat actors create new accounts not previously on the device. In certain cases, threat actors
        delete existing accounts to block organizations from accessing affected devices and to maintain
        persistence on the system while attempting lateral movement within the environment.

        Indicators of Compromise
        IP Addresses in Victim Logs
        Initial responses revealed ongoing scanning activity on exposed Fortinet firewalls. Threat actors use a
        combination of Internet Protocol (IP) addresses for their infrastructure to include their Command and
        Control (C2) servers, Relay Nodes, performing scanning activities, and using Hashtopolis. IP address
        45.154.12.132 was seen in instances as a C2 server, with IP addresses 154.202.59.169 and
        103.27.186.156 as proxy nodes used by the threat actors to obfuscate their presence, and
        45.155.250.158 as a beacon relay. Threat actors use multiple different ports, including 4332 and 4432,
        but usually beaconing to (Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure) HTTPS. In addition, IP addresses
        193.8.187.2, 193.8.187.42, and 85.11.187.8 were seen facilitating the FortiBleed attack chain, with IP
        address 85.11.187.8 being used for the Hashtopolis tool.4 The following IP addresses have been identified

        Incident Response
        If a potential compromise is detected, organizations should take the following actions:
             1. Determine which hosts were compromised and isolated them by quarantining or taking them
                offline.
             2. Initiate threat hunting activities to scope the intrusion—collect and review relevant artifacts, logs,
                and other data to identify threat actor TTPs, compromised devices and accounts, a timeline of
                activity, etc.
             3. Report the compromise to the FBI, USSS, and other agencies as appropriate (see Contact
                Information for details).
             4. Apply eviction countermeasures, including those listed below, to contain the incident and eradicate
                the threat actor from the network (Note: Start applying countermeasures after collecting enough
                threat hunting data to inform effective countermeasure selection; this will likely overlap with threat
                hunting activities):
                 a. Use CISA’s Eviction Strategies Tool to assemble countermeasures for a systematic eviction
                    plan—the tool comprises Playbook-NG (a web application) and COUN7ER (a database of post-
                    compromise countermeasures mapped to adversary TTPs).
                     i.   Use Playbook-NG and COUN7ER together to assemble a systematic eviction plan, or
                          playbook, that leverages distinct countermeasures to contain and evict cyber threat actors.
                          The playbook features a list of recommended response actions based on threat actor TTPs
                          and includes each action’s intended outcome, preparatory steps, and associated risks. For
                          more information, see CISA’s Eviction Strategies Tool Fact Sheet.
             5. Harden the network to prevent additional malicious activity (see Mitigations for guidance).

        Mitigations
        Due to the potential damage this attack chain can lead to with lockout and ransomware, the FBI and USSS
        encourage organizations to review the following mitigations to help defend against this malicious cyber
        activity, impacting all Fortinet customers with exposed gateways:5
             ▪   Reduce your attack surface and lock down management access. Restrict external management of
                 your devices via trusted hosts (good), a local-in policy (better), or remove internet administration
                 altogether (best).
             ▪   Terminate all admin and VPN sessions and reset credentials. Terminate all active administrative
                 sessions. Reset all Fortinet VPN and administrative passwords, especially on internet-facing
                 systems, and enforce strong password policies.
             ▪   Enable phishing-resistant multifactor authentication (MFA). Require phishing-resistant MFA on all
                 remote access and administrative accounts and ensure it is enforced on all external gateways and
                 administrative interfaces.
             ▪   Validate configuration. Review firewall and VPN users and other configuration for unauthorized
                 changes. Preferably, compare configurations to a well-known configuration. Pay particular attention
                 to the addition of unrecognized accounts. A list of well-known compromised account names has
                 been mentioned in this report.
             ▪   Review logs. Review firewall, VPN, authentication, and domain controller logs for lateral movement,
                 unusual access, suspicious accounts, or unauthorized configuration changes. A list of commonly
                 identified IP addresses has been referenced in this report to assist with a review.
             ▪   Ensure secure credential storage. Confirm your organization’s use of the Password-Based Key
                 Derivation Function 2 (PBKDF2) algorithm to store administrator credentials and remove weaker
                 legacy hashes per Fortinet’s guidance. Refer to Fortinet's technical guidance on enforcing PBKDF2
                 as hash function for administrator accounts in FortiOS v7.2.11 and later.6

        The authoring organizations recommend organizations investigate or vet IOCs prior to acting, such as
        blocking.



        REST APIs
        Representational state transfer (REST) application programming interface (API) keys can enable automated
        configuration, backup creation, and monitoring of FortiGate firewalls.7 Any unknown or unexpected keys
        may present a significant security risk. All API keys should be reviewed to confirm they have a valid purpose
        in the environment. It is recommended to identify and remove any unknown API keys and refresh all
        legitimate keys to ensure threat actors cannot leverage them for access.

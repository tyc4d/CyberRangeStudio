        Advisory at a Glance
          Title                        #StopRansomware: SafePay Ransomware

          Original Publication         October 20, 2026

          Executive Summary            This advisory details recent activity by the SafePay ransomware group. SafePay
                                       actors have been highly active since August 2024, targeting businesses and
                                       critical infrastructure across North America, South America, Europe, and Australia.
                                       SafePay actors typically gain access to networks by stealing or guessing
                                       passwords, exploiting vulnerabilities in security systems, and using phishing
                                       tactics. Once inside the network, SafePay actors establish control by creating new
                                       accounts or taking over existing ones. They then use legitimate system tools to
                                       move through the network, identify valuable files, and disable security protections
                                       to avoid detection. Next, they encrypt the victim’s files, making them inaccessible,
                                       and leave a ransom note demanding payment in cryptocurrency. This advisory
                                       urges organizations to follow recommended security measures to reduce the risk
                                       and impact of SafePay ransomware attacks.

        Introduction
        Note: This joint Cybersecurity Advisory is part of an ongoing #StopRansomware effort to publish advisories
        for network defenders that detail various ransomware variants and ransomware threat actors. These
        #StopRansomware advisories include recently and historically observed tactics, techniques, and
        procedures (TTPs) and indicators of compromise (IOCs) to help organizations protect against ransomware.
        Visit stopransomware.gov to see all #StopRansomware advisories and to learn more about other
        ransomware threats and no-cost resources.
        The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), United
        States Secret Service (USSS), Germany’s Landeskriminalamt Rheinland-Pfalz, the United Kingdom’s
        National Crime Agency, and the United Kingdom’s North West Regional Organised Crime Unit—hereafter
        referred to as the authoring agencies—are releasing this joint advisory to disseminate known SafePay
        ransomware TTPs and IOCs identified through FBI, USSS, and Landeskriminalamt Rheinland-Pfalz
        investigations as recently as August 2026.
        Since August 2024, SafePay ransomware group’s operations have affected a wide range of businesses
        and critical infrastructure in North America, South America, Europe, and Australia. SafePay ransomware
        was among the most active ransomware groups in 2026. The SafePay threat actors have impacted at least
        600 separate victims from multiple critical infrastructure sectors.
        The authoring agencies encourage organizations to implement the recommendations in the Mitigations
        section of this advisory to reduce the likelihood and impact of SafePay ransomware incidents.
        For a downloadable copy of IOCs, see:

        Technical Details
        Note: This advisory uses the MITRE ATT&CK® Matrix for Enterprise framework, version 20. See MITRE
        ATT&CK Tactics and Techniques section of this advisory for a table of the threat actors’ activity mapped to
        MITRE ATT&CK tactics and techniques.

        Initial Access
        The FBI, USSS, and cybersecurity researchers observed SafePay threat actors obtaining initial access
        [TA0001] to organizations through various means, including credential exposure, implementing brute force
        password attacks [T1110], and exploiting virtual private network (VPN) access. SafePay threat actors use
        the IP addresses of the VPN provider “Mullvad” to attain initial access—specifically, SafePay threat actors
        use phished account profiles to access victim networks to exploit the following known Common
        Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) for edge device products:
                CVE-2020-3259 [Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE)-200: Exposure of Sensitive Information
                 to an Unauthorized Actor]
                CVE-2023-20269 [CWE-288: Authentication Bypass Using an Alternate Path or Channel]

        Execution, Persistence, Stealth, and Defense Impairment
        After SafePay threat actors gain access to a victim network, they establish persistence by gaining
        administrative privileges on existing accounts [T1098], or on newly created accounts [T1136], using built-
        in administrative tools consistent with living off the land (LOTL) techniques. Once SafePay threat actors
        establish a foothold in a victim’s network, they use LOTL binaries (LOLBins) [T1218], such as PsExec
        [T1021.002] and Windows Remote Management (WinRM) [T1021.006], to execute commands on remote
        systems. In several instances, SafePay threat actors used ShareFinder.ps1—a PowerShell script function
        used for enumerating network file shares in Windows Active Directory environments—to identify valuable
        assets for exfiltration [T1059.001, T1059.003]. Additionally, SafePay threat actors used
        SharpShares.exe, a tool used to identify and enumerate network file shares.
        As SafePay threat actors prepare for lateral movement, they commonly disable security software [T1685]
        to avoid detection. In some cases, the threat actors changed the passwords of system users, making valid
        logins impossible. SafePay threat actors have also been observed using software packing or virtual
        machine software protection to conceal their code [T1027.002].

        Privilege Escalation
        The authoring agencies observed SafePay threat actors using a User Account Control (UAC) Bypass
        Privilege Escalation technique [T1548.002] that creates an elevated process through a specified
        component object model, enabling the execution of malicious commands or binaries [T1059]. SafePay
        threat actors have also been observed using token impersonation methods [T1134.001].

        Lateral Movement
        To pivot laterally through a network and identify files for exfiltration [TA0010] and encryption, SafePay
        threat actors use legitimate tools like PsExec, WinRM, and Remote Management and Monitoring
        [T1569.002] software. They also employ Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) [T1021.001, T1133] to expand
        their presence within the compromised network [T1021]. As SafePay threat actors move laterally through a
        network, they look for credentials to gain admin access and will create accounts, if needed.

        Collection, Exfiltration, and Impact
        SafePay threat actors use legitimate tools, such as FileZilla, 7-Zip, RClone, and WinRAR to compress files
        and collect data [T1560.001], and WinSCP and RClone to facilitate the exfiltration of data [T1048] from
        the compromised network to actor-controlled accounts. In some instances, SafePay threat actors use
        OneDrive to exfiltrate data [T1567]. In other instances, the SafePay threat actors installed legitimate
        remote desktop tools like AnyDesk or TeamViewer on the victim’s systems. SafePay threat actors use these
        tools to transfer ransomware and other software onto a victim’s system. Additionally, SafePay threat actors

        halt the Volume Shadow Copy Service and reset Network Attached Storage containing victim backup data
        to initial factory settings to inhibit system recovery attempts [T1490].
        Following exfiltration, SafePay threat actors encrypt files [T1486] using a sophisticated hybrid encryption
        scheme to lock data. This involves combining a ChaCha20 stream cipher with an RSA public-key
        cryptosystem for a fast and secure key exchange. This multilayered approach customizes encryption
        methods based on file type and size and enables full or partial encryption. Data encrypted with the SafePay
        ransomware variant (appended with .SafePay) cannot be recovered without the decryption key. SafePay
        threat actors hold the encrypted data for ransom and typically demand a subset of company revenue in
        cryptocurrency in exchange for decryption software. SafePay threat actors leave a ransom note on the
        victim’s system, typically on the desktop or root directory, advising them that SafePay locked their system
        and to reach out over a chat portal at a TOR .onion website link with a victim ID as the login. To put
        pressure on the victim, a representative makes phone calls on behalf of the ransomware group. If the
        victim does not pay, SafePay posts the exfiltrated data to their SafePay leak website, which is linked in the
        ransom note. Following encryption, SafePay may delete itself from the victim’s filesystem [T1070.004].

        Incident Response
        If a potential compromise is detected, but ransomware actors have not (yet) encrypted items, organizations
        should take the following actions:
             1. Determine which hosts were compromised and isolate them by quarantining or taking them offline.
             2. Initiate threat hunting activities to scope the intrusion. Collect and review relevant artifacts, logs,
                and other data to identify threat actor TTPs, compromised devices and accounts, a timeline of
                activity, etc.
             3. Report the suspected compromise to the FBI and other agencies as appropriate (if located within
                the U.K., report the compromise to Report Fraud or local police force) (see the Reporting section for
                contact information).
             4. Apply eviction countermeasures, including those listed below, to contain the incident and eradicate
                the threat actor from the network (Note: Start applying countermeasures after collecting enough
                threat hunting data to inform effective countermeasure selection; this will likely overlap with threat
                hunting activities).
                 a. Use CISA’s Eviction Strategies Tool to assemble countermeasures for a systematic eviction
                    plan—the tool comprises Playbook-NG (a web application) and COUN7ER (a database of post-
                    compromise countermeasures mapped to adversary TTPs).

        Mitigations
        The authoring agencies recommend organizations implement the mitigations below to improve their
        cybersecurity posture on the basis of the threat actors’ activity. These mitigations align with the Cross-
        Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals (CPGs) developed by CISA and the National Institute of Standards
        and Technology (NIST). The CPGs provide a minimum set of practices and protections that CISA and NIST
        recommend all organizations implement. CISA and NIST based the CPGs on existing cybersecurity
        frameworks and guidance to protect against the most common and impactful threats, tactics, techniques,
        and procedures. Visit CISA’s CPGs webpage for more information on the CPGs, including additional
        recommended baseline protections.
        Organizations should implement the following mitigations:
                Prioritize remediating known exploited vulnerabilities (KEVs) and keep all operating systems,
                 software, and firmware up to date, as timely patching is one of the most efficient and cost-effective
                 steps an organization can take to minimize its exposure to cybersecurity threats; specifically,
                 prioritize patching KEVs in internet-facing systems. [CPG 2.B].
                 o    Additionally, using Binding Operational Directive 26-04 offers a framework for patch
                      prioritization based on technology type and steps for forensics triage. Under its implementation
                      guidance, it offers guidance and steps to perform forensic triage for severe vulnerability cases.
                Implement identity, credential, and access management (ICAM) policies across the organization
                 and require MFA for all services to the greatest extent possible—particularly for webmail, VPNs, and
                 accounts that access critical systems [CPG 3.F].
                Require all accounts with password logins (e.g., service accounts, admin accounts, and domain
                 admin accounts) to comply with NIST’s digital identity guidelines; require employees to use long
                 passwords and consider not requiring recurring password changes, as these can weaken security
                 [CPG 3.B].
                 o    Use longer passwords consisting of at least 15 characters and no more than 64 characters in
                      length.
                 o    Store passwords in a hashed format using industry-recognized password managers.
                 o    Add password user “salts” to shared login credentials.
                 o    Avoid reusing passwords [CPG 3.C].
                 o    Implement multiple failed login attempt account lockouts [CPG 3.E].

                 o    Disable password “hints.”
                 o    Refrain from requiring password changes more frequently than once per year.
                      Note: NIST guidance suggests favoring longer passwords instead of requiring frequent
                      password resets. Frequent password resets are more likely to result in users developing
                      password patterns cyber threat actors can easily decipher.
                Maintain offline backups of data and regularly test and maintain backup and restoration processes
                 [CPG 3.O]; implementing this practice helps organizations avoid severe operational interruptions
                 and prevents scenarios in which data is irretrievable.
                Ensure all backup data is encrypted, immutable (i.e., cannot be altered or deleted), and covers the
                 entire organization’s data infrastructure [CPG 3.K].
                Implement a recovery plan to maintain and retain multiple copies of sensitive or proprietary data
                 and servers in a physically separate, segmented, and secure location (e.g., hard drives, storage
                 devices, or the cloud) [CPG 3.I, 3.O, 1.C].
                Segment networks to prevent the spread of ransomware, as network segmentation can help
                 control traffic flows between, and access to, various subnetworks and restrict adversary lateral
                 movement [CPG 3.I].
                Identify, detect, and investigate abnormal activity and potential traversal of the indicated
                 ransomware with a network monitoring tool [CPG 4.B].
                 o    To aid in detecting ransomware, implement a tool that logs and reports all network traffic,
                      including lateral movement activity on a network.
                 o    Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) tools are particularly useful for detecting lateral
                      connections as they have insight into common and uncommon network connections for each
                      host.
                Filter network traffic by preventing unknown or untrusted origins from accessing remote services
                 on internal systems—this prevents threat actors from directly connecting to remote access services
                 that they have established for persistence.
                Review domain controllers, servers, workstations, and Active Directories for new, unactive and/or
                 unrecognized accounts.
                Disable unused ports.
                Consider adding an email banner to emails received from outside of your organization.
                Audit user accounts with administrative privileges and configure access controls according to the
                 principle of least privilege [CPG 3.G].
                Implement time-based access for accounts set at the admin level and higher; for example, the Just-
                 in-Time (JIT) access method provisions privileged access when needed and can support
                 enforcement of the principle of least privilege (as well as the Zero Trust Maturity Model).
                 o    This is a process where a network-wide policy is set in place to automatically disable admin
                      accounts at the Active Directory level when the account is not in direct need.

                 o    Individual users may submit their requests through an automated process that grants them
                      access to a specified system for a set timeframe when they need to support the completion of
                      a certain task.
                Disable command-line and scripting activities and permissions, since privilege escalation and
                 lateral movement often depend on software utilities running from the command line; if threat
                 actors cannot run these tools, they will have difficulty escalating privileges and moving laterally
                 [CPG 3.M].

        These mitigations align with principles provided in the joint guide Shifting the Balance of Cybersecurity
        Risk: Principles and Approaches for Secure by Design Software. CISA urges software manufacturers to take
        ownership of improving the security outcomes of their customers by applying these and other secure by
        design tactics. By implementing secure by design tactics, software manufacturers can make their product
        lines secure “out of the box” without requiring customers to make additional configuration changes,
        purchasing product security add-in software and logs, monitoring, and making frequent security updates.

        Validate Security Controls
        In addition to applying mitigations, the authoring agencies recommend exercising, testing, and validating
        your organization's security program against the threat behaviors mapped to the MITRE ATT&CK for
        Enterprise framework in this advisory.
        To get started:
             1. Select an ATT&CK technique described in this advisory (see Table 6 to Table 16).
             2. Align your security technologies against the technique.
             3. Test your technologies against the technique.
             4. Analyze your detection and prevention technologies’ performance.
             5. Repeat the process for all security technologies to obtain a set of comprehensive performance
                data.
             6. Tune your security program, including people, processes, and technologies, based on the data
                generated by this process.
        The authoring agencies recommend continually testing your security program, at scale, in a production
        environment to ensure optimal performance against the MITRE ATT&CK techniques identified in this
        advisory.

        Reporting
        Your organization has no obligation to respond or provide information to the FBI and other authoring
        agencies in response to this joint advisory. If, after reviewing the information provided, your organization
        decides to provide information to the FBI or other authoring agencies, reporting must be consistent with
        applicable state and federal laws.
        The FBI and other authoring agencies are interested in any information, to include boundary logs showing
        communication to and from foreign IP addresses, a sample ransom note, communications with threat
        actors, Bitcoin wallet information, decryptor files, and/or a benign sample of an encrypted file.
        Additional details of interest include a targeted company point of contact, status and scope of infection,
        estimated loss, operational impact, transaction IDs, date of infection, date detected, initial attack vector,
        and host- and network-based indicators.
        The authoring agencies do not encourage paying ransom as payment does not guarantee victim files will
        be recovered. Furthermore, payment may also embolden adversaries to target additional organizations,
        encourage other criminal actors to engage in the distribution of ransomware, and/or fund illicit activities.
        Regardless of whether you or your organization have decided to pay the ransom, the FBI and CISA urge you
